Twenty Minutes a Day, Twenty Years of Protection: What the ACTIVE Study Tells Us About Brain Training and Dementia


What if a few weeks of playing a computer game could protect your brain for the next two decades? A landmark study published in February 2026 suggests it might be exactly what the science is showing.  

Researchers from Johns Hopkins published the longest follow-up study of its kind in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia: Translational Research & Clinical Interventions. Their findings come from the ACTIVE trial (Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly), which tracked over 2,800 adults aged 65 and older who participated in a cognitive training program back in 1998. Two decades later, researchers linked those participants to their Medicare records to see who had been diagnosed with dementia.  

The results were striking. Participants in the speed-training group who completed booster sessions had a 25% lower rate of dementia diagnosis than those who received no training (40% versus 49%).  

Not all training worked equally, though. Memory and reasoning training produced no statistically significant reduction in dementia risk, suggesting that the type of training matters just as much as doing it at all. Speed-of-processing training challenges the brain to quickly identify visual information on a screen with tasks that grow harder over time. This was the only format that showed lasting benefit. Consistency mattered: participants who completed follow-up booster sessions maintained their gains, while those who skipped them did not.  

Researchers at Johns Hopkins described the findings as remarkable, noting that a relatively modest, non-drug intervention appeared capable of producing protective effects that lasted two full decades.  

The takeaway is straightforward. Keeping your brain active in the right ways, with regular reinforcement, has real consequences for your long-term health.
